Hi all,

I was bit dismayed to see the vibe coding policy change Replace blanket AI ban with disclosure-based policy (#641) · flathub-infra/documentation@43e6e15 · GitHub .

I feel like vibe coding, for the time being at least, is fundamentally incompatible with the ideals of free software and am keen to avoid it.

The spectrum between I’m good engineer who used some AI auto-complete and I rewrote Photoshop without having any idea what the AI was doing PhotoCraft: The image editor you already know how to use. — ArtCraft is pretty broad but the disclosure doesn’t really have the nuance the decipher between the two.

Further, just looking at app quality isn’t sufficient either - as some vibe code apps look good and function well but the source is generally an inscrutable disaster.

If I need a giant AI running in the cloud to make modifications to a piece of code because it’s so convoluted it might as well be machine language, then it’s not free. It’s slop as a software substitute and I’d rather not be dependent on it.

More worryingly perhaps, I feel like we’re on the cusp of fork-pocalypse. Every project that took a stand against vibe coding is going to get forked and enslopified. Or worse, it won’t get forked, the AI launder the original code into a new codebase, and a new less principled dev will run with it. End user’s will be wooed by fast features thereby robbing many real projects from their userbase.

Again, I realize this is a challenging moment with lots of squishy factors that complicate the policy making so I’m not sure there’s a good answer for the time being. But I was very cheered that Flathub seemed to be taking a strong stance against this stuff and would really like to see the stronger policy reinstated.

That’s unfortunately by design to some degree. It’s been litigated on the Internet for time eternal - and I’m sure you know most the history - but “open source” was largely a systematic neutering of the free software movement to make it more broadly palatable.

As a kid in the mid 90s I ran Debian on the first computer my parents bought for me and grew up reading Eric Raymond and Jon Hall. In the early 2000s I switch to a Macintosh because I viewed computing as basically pragmatic - use what works - and the Mac looked and works a heck of a lot better than what I was used to at the time. In the mid 2000s Stallman gave a talk at my University that open my eyes to the broader political machinations of software freedom but I came away thinking he was kinda kooky. It wasn’t until a few years later working for software company that was building the worst kind of anti-features that I started recognizing how right he was about a great many things.

Exclusion-to-enforce-inclusion is a difficult concept for people and those watching uncritically tend to find those sorts of efforts tyrannical. And, of course, bad actors will take advantage of that to promote their toxic causes. I think we need to start thinking about spaces and mechanisms to enforce human authorship in perpetuity - like a GPLv4 - or a human only store front. Let Omarchy and Windows, and the other long list of lost causes have the hardcore vibe coders I think. But I totally understand and respect Flathub’s stance.

It is quite remarkable, given how many GNU/Linux adjacent projects I’ve seen turn into corporate land-grabs how grass roots Flathub still is. This moment has placed a larger burden on you all than anyone probably expected, but I’m really grateful there’s a thoughtful group of people working through these issues. I didn’t see sponsorship option on Github for the other devs you mentioned but I’d be happy to support them similarly.

Replying to #7

Personally, I am glad to see the AI policy loosened. Speaking as someone with obvious biases (snap maintainer, and I work in the AI-adjacent space), I think the bigger problem is tooling and maintainer bandwidth, and AI is part of the fix rather than the threat.

Two recent examples.

calibre: flathubbot opened the 9.15 update PR on 19 September. It’s still open. 9.16 shipped on Friday and there’s a second PR for that too. Nobody’s doing anything wrong here. There just aren’t enough hands, and calibre isn’t an obscure app.

Symbolical: I helped the developer get their manifest through review. Their PR was closed as AI slop, which it wasn’t. Another maintainer stepped in, sorted it out, and it got there in the end, so credit where it’s due. But for a first-time publisher, that’s a rough introduction. I also wrote their snap packaging, and getting the same app into the Snap Store was quicker and a lot less stressful. I’m not mentioning this as a sleight, or them-vs-us, just fact observed by the publisher.

I don’t think that’s news to anyone here, and I know time and people are the constraint, not willingness.

Two things that really need help from the community:

  1. Recruit more maintainers. Hard, unglamorous, and the only long-term answer. A visible “apps needing a co-maintainer” list would help people who want to contribute but don’t know where.
  2. Automate more of the review, not just the PR. The bot already spots new releases and opens PRs. The slow bit is a human looking at a green build and deciding it’s safe. A pre-review pass could summarise the diff, read the build log, flag permission or runtime changes, and label routine version bumps as low-risk. Run it on an open-weights model if that’s a concern. Maintainers still press the button, but they’d only need to look closely at the PRs that warrant it. The same pass could give first-time submitters early, specific feedback, before a human gets involved and before anyone has to guess how the manifest was written.

Without something like this, the backlog grows, the same few people carry it, and sadly they will likely burn out.

Hi popey -

Thanks for your thoughts - the maintainers here are free to do what works for them obviously.

Speaking for myself, I think you’ve touched on one of my primary concerns around AI adoption. It seems to be innately cancerous. People at my workpace are starting to use it for code reviews - and I understand it we’re all busy - but there’s something undeniably dehumanizing about being dropped a mass of text and then told to sort through it. Indeed the AI sometimes picks up on something pertinent, but just as often it’s drivel or pointing things out without fulling grokking the implications and now the submitter has to sort through it.

It’s worse when AI is used to modify code bases. The code becomes genuinely human-hostile. The 1000 file change-sets can’t be reviewed effectively without AI and suddenly everyone has to use it or be left behind. Once you let a little bit in here or there it spreads and ultimately crowds the humans to the periphery.

Obviously, there’s an inevitability to this for most projects in most places. Vibe coders will soon outnumber “agentic engineers”, will outnumber traditional developers. And that’s fine I guess, it’s the way of so-called progress.

But code review can be incredibly edifying if done with care. It’s uniquely human meeting of minds. Indeed the relationship between a software author and user has traditionally been something similar if a bit more abstracted. Hackers are as much painters as they are engineers and it’s important that some groups take a hard stand to maintain that.
